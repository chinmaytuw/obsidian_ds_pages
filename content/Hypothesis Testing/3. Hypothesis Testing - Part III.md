# Scenario:
1. We have a heuristic model and an ML model. Heuristic is exposed to 4mln players and ML to 1mln.
2. Heuristic has conversion rate of 0.05 i.e. 5% and ML has 6%.
3. Null hypothesis is that there is no difference between the 2 models.
4. The objective is to calculate statistical significance of the results.

# Solution
## Step 1: Pooled proportion $\hat p$
- Typically, in one sample #z_test , as we saw in the classic examples in [[2. Hypothesis Testing - Part II]], we know the population mean. We compare our sample mean to this population mean to calculate the #z_statistic  and #p_value .
- In this scenario, we do not know the population mean. So our next best bet is to calculate a pooled conversion rate. This is a weighted average of the conversion rates. Here, n is number of players and p is conversion rate. 
	$\hat p​=\frac{n1​⋅p1​+n2​⋅p2}{n1+n2}​​$ 
	$\hat p​=\frac{1M​⋅0.06​+5M​⋅0.05}{1M+4M}​​$ 
	$\hat p = \frac{60k+200k}{5M}$
	$\hat p = 0.052$
- We are assuming that under #null_hypothesis , both conversions came from the same population. If we are to prove that ML is better, we need to prove it came from a different population.
- We're not measuring how far each conversion rate is from the pooled mean individually.
	- We're measuring: **"<mark style="background: #BBFABBA6;">How far apart are the two conversion rates from EACH OTHER?</mark>"**
	- The z-score tells us: "<mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">If they came from the same population, how surprising is it to see them this far apart?</mark>"
- The pooled proportion's role is to be a reference point to calculate the **standard error** (how much random variation to expect), not as a comparison point itself.
---
## Step 2: Standard Error #standard_error 

The formula:
$SE = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}$

Where:
- $\hat{p} = 0.052$ (our pooled proportion)
- $n_1 = 1,000,000$ (ML sample size)
- $n_2 = 4,000,000$ (Heuristic sample size)

Let's calculate:
$SE = \sqrt{0.052 \times 0.948 \times \left(\frac{1}{1,000,000} + \frac{1}{4,000,000}\right)}$
$SE = \sqrt{0.049296 \times (0.000001 + 0.00000025)}$
$SE = \sqrt{0.049296 \times 0.00000125}$
$SE = \sqrt{0.00000006162} = 0.000248$

- So SE ≈ 0.000248 or 0.0248%
- This means random variation alone could cause differences of about ±0.025% between the two models.


## Step 3: Z score
**Perfect! Now we calculate the #z_score :

$z = \frac{p_1 - p_2}{SE} = \frac{0.06 - 0.05}{0.000248}$
$z = \frac{0.01}{0.000248} = 40.32$

**What does z = 40.32 mean?**
- The two conversion rates are **40.32 standard errors apart**.
- In other words: if these models were truly the same, the probability of seeing a 1% difference (or larger) with these sample sizes is essentially **zero**.

For context:
- z > 1.96 → statistically significant at α = 0.05
	- **$1.96$** is the threshold for a **two-tailed** test at $\alpha = 0.05$. For a **one-tailed** test (just checking if ML is _better_), the threshold is actually lower, at **$1.645$
- z > 2.58 → statistically significant at α = 0.01
- z = 40.32 → **astronomically significant**


## Step 4: P value

- The #p_value  answers: **"If the null hypothesis were true (models are the same), what's the probability of seeing a difference this extreme or more extreme?"**

**Converting z to p-value:**
- We look up z = 40.32 in the standard normal distribution table, or use a calculator.
- For z = 40.32, the p-value is essentially **p < 0.0000000001** (many more zeros actually!)
- For practical purposes: **p < 0.0001** or even **p < 10⁻³⁰⁰** (it's ridiculously small)

**Interpretation:**
- There's less than a 0.00000001% chance we'd see ML at 6% and Heuristic at 5% if they were actually the same.

**Compare to our significance level:**
- We set α = 0.05 (5%)
- Our p-value ≪ 0.05
- Therefore: **Reject the null hypothesis!**

**Conclusion: The ML model is statistically significantly better than the heuristic.**


## Step 5: Confidence intervals
#confidence_interval 

- Calculating the Confidence Interval (CI) gives you the "precision" of your result, while understanding "peeking" (or the salami problem) prevents you from being fooled by random noise.

### 5.1 The 95% Confidence Interval (CI)

- While your z-score of $40.32$ tells us the 1% difference is "real", the CI tells us exactly how much better the ML model is likely to be.
- **Note on Formula:** For a CI, we use the standard error of the **difference**, which is slightly different from the "pooled" SE you used for the z-test. The pooled SE assumes the groups are the same; the CI SE uses each group's individual variance.

$$SE_{diff} = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}$$

**The Calculation:**
- **$p_1$ (ML):** $0.06$ with $n_1 = 1,000,000$
- **$p_2$ (Heuristic):** $0.05$ with $n_2 = 4,000,000$
- **Difference ($d$):** $0.01$ (or 1%)
    

Using the formula:

$$SE_{diff} = \sqrt{\frac{0.06 \times 0.94}{1,000,000} + \frac{0.05 \times 0.95}{4,000,000}} \approx 0.000261$$

For a 95% confidence level, we multiply the SE by $1.96$:

$$Margin~of~Error = 1.96 \times 0.000261 \approx 0.00051$$

**The Result:** Your 95% CI is **$1\% \pm 0.05\%$**. In plain English: We are 95% confident that the ML model is between **$0.95\%$** and **$1.05\%$** better than the heuristic. This is a very tight range, which is why your result is so "astronomically significant".

# Side note #1: What does the normal distribution mean here?
## **Question**: 
in the given example, we calculate the z score and then the p value. This is all assuming a normal distribution. What does that distribution represent? Is it suggesting that if we run the experiments multiple times, we would get a normal distribution of conversion rates? Or something else?
## **Reply**:
- You’ve hit on one of the most important concepts in statistics: the **Sampling Distribution**.
- To answer your question directly: **Yes, it is the "something else."** The normal distribution does not represent the individual players, nor does it represent a distribution of the conversion rates themselves.
It represents the **Sampling Distribution of the Difference in Proportions.**

---

### 1. What is actually being "distributed"?

In your scenario, an individual player either converts (1) or doesn't (0). That’s a Bernoulli distribution, not a normal one. However, the **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** tells us that if we take large samples ($n$), the _average_ of those samples (the proportion) will follow a normal distribution.

If you were to run this exact experiment 10,000 times—assuming the Null Hypothesis is true (meaning there is actually **zero** difference between the models)—and you plotted the **difference** ($p_1 - p_2$) from each experiment on a graph:

- The **center** of your bell curve would be at **0** (because $H_0$ says the difference is zero).
- The **width** of that bell curve is determined by your **Standard Error** ($0.000248$).
- Most of those 10,000 experiments would show a difference very close to 0.

---

### 2. How your Z-score fits in

Your specific result was a difference of **1%** ($0.01$). When you calculate the $z$-score, you are essentially asking:

> "If the 'True' difference is 0, where does my 1% result fall on that bell curve of 10,000 hypothetical experiments?"

Because your Standard Error is so tiny ($0.0248\%$), a 1% difference is **40.32 standard deviations** away from the center. On a standard bell curve, almost all data (99.7%) falls within 3 standard deviations. Being 40 deviations away means your result is so far out in the "tail" that it's practically on another planet.

### 3. Why Sample Size ($n$) matters

This is why your $N$ of 5 million is so powerful.

- If you had only 100 players, your "bell curve" of random noise would be very wide. A 1% difference would be buried in the middle of the noise.
- Because you have 5 million players, the "bell curve" of random noise is incredibly skinny. This makes even a small 1% difference stand out like a lighthouse.

---

### Summary Table: What is Normal?

|**Level**|**Distribution Type**|**What it represents**|
|---|---|---|
|**Individual Player**|Bernoulli (0 or 1)|Did this specific person buy something?|
|**The Experiment**|**Sampling Distribution**|If we ran this 1M vs 4M test again, what _difference_ would we likely see?|
|**The Result**|Z-Test|How "weird" is our 1% difference compared to that Sampling Distribution?|

### Why the result changed

Using the formula from your notes:

$$SE = \sqrt{0.052 \times 0.948 \times (\frac{1}{1000} + \frac{1}{4000})} \approx 0.00785$$

1. **The "Noise" exploded:** In your original notes, random variation only caused a difference of about $\pm0.025\%$. With the small sample, random variation can easily cause a difference of $\pm0.785\%$.
2. **The 1% difference is now "normal":** In the big test, a 1% lift was a miracle (40 standard deviations away). In the small test, a 1% lift is only **1.27 standard deviations** away.
3. **The Verdict:** A z-score of $1.27$ is well below the $1.96$ threshold for significance. If you saw a 1% difference in a sample of 5,000, you’d have to say, _"This might just be luck. I need more data."_

---

### Visualization: The "Skinny" vs. "Fat" Curve

Imagine the normal distribution we discussed.

- **With 5M players:** The bell curve is a tiny, needle-thin spike. The 1% result is miles away from that spike.
- **With 5K players:** The bell curve is a wide, floppy hill. The 1% result is sitting right on the shoulder of that hill, perfectly comfortable and expected under the Null Hypothesis.

This is why big companies love "Big Data"—it allows them to prove that a $0.1\%$ increase in revenue is "real" and not just a fluke, whereas a small startup could never prove that same $0.1\%$ is anything other than noise.



# Power Calculation
- Calculating the required sample size (often called "Power Calculation") is how you ensure your experiment doesn't fail simply because it was too small. In your original notes, you had **5 million players**, which resulted in a massive z-score of **$40.32$**.
In reality, you could have reached a conclusion much sooner.

---

### 1. The Four Pillars of Power

To calculate how many players you need, you must balance four variables:

- **Significance ($\alpha$):** The risk of a "False Positive." Typically set at **$0.05$**. 
- **Power ($1 - \beta$):** The probability of finding a "winner" if one exists. The industry standard is **$0.80$** (an 80% chance).
- **Baseline Conversion ($p_1$):** Your current rate, which was **$5\%$** ($0.05$).
- **Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE):** The smallest lift you care about. In your case, that was a **$1\%$** absolute lift ($0.06 - 0.05$).

---

### 2. The Sample Size Formula

For comparing two proportions ($p_1$ and $p_2$), the formula for the number of subjects _per group_ ($n$) is:

$$n = \frac{(Z_{\alpha/2} + Z_{\beta})^2 \cdot [p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)]}{(p_1 - p_2)^2}$$

#### Step-by-Step with your numbers:

1. **Find the Z-values:**
    - For $\alpha = 0.05$, $Z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96$.
    - For $80\%$ Power ($\beta = 0.20$), $Z_{\beta} \approx 0.84$.
        
2. **Calculate the Variance part:**
    
    - $p_1(1-p_1) = 0.05 \times 0.95 = 0.0475$
    - $p_2(1-p_2) = 0.06 \times 0.94 = 0.0564$
    - Total variance $= 0.1039$
        
3. **Calculate the denominator (Effect size squared):**
    
    - $(0.06 - 0.05)^2 = 0.01^2 = 0.0001$
        
4. **Solve for $n$:**
    
    - $n = \frac{(1.96 + 0.84)^2 \cdot 0.1039}{0.0001}$
    - $n = \frac{7.84 \cdot 0.1039}{0.0001} \approx \mathbf{8,146}$ **players per group.**

---

### 3. Why this matters for your 5M player test

Your test used **1,000,000** players for the ML model and **4,000,000** for the Heuristic.

- **The "Overkill" Factor:** You only needed about **8,200** players per group to have an 80% chance of proving the ML model was better.
- **The Benefit:** By using millions of players, your Power was effectively **100%**. This is why your p-value was "astronomically" small.
- **The Trade-off:** In a business setting, running a test on 5 million people when you only need 16,000 is "expensive." If the Heuristic model was actually hurting the user experience, you "wasted" 4 million players on a worse version for longer than necessary.

---

### Summary Table: Sample Size Needs

If you wanted to detect a **smaller** lift with the same $5\%$ baseline:

|**Target Lift (MDE)**|**Required n (per group)**|**Total Players Needed**|
|---|---|---|
|**$1.0\%$** (Your Case)|$\approx 8,200$|$16,400$|
|**$0.5\%$**|$\approx 31,000$|$62,000$|
|**$0.1\%$**|$\approx 750,000$|$1,500,000$|
